---
title: The Lamplighter
date: 2026-06-14
description: "Half my life as a paid engineer — and the shift that interests me most: learning to work with AI as a partner, not a rival."
---

I built my first website at eleven. At thirteen, someone paid me to do it again. By sixteen I'd started calling myself an engineer, and I've never really stopped — across more fields than I ever expected to touch. I won't tell you my age, but I'll tell you this: I've been a paid engineer for a very long time.

In that time I've worked just about every way there is to work — employed, contracted, freelance, ad-hoc, on my own ventures, and a few arrangements I'll politely call clandestine (nothing illegal, I promise). The titles changed, the clients changed, the stack changed more times than I can count. One thing never did: I love writing code. Scripts, tools, whole applications — the act of building something that works is, to me, its own reward.

Five years ago, every project I touched — paid or hobby, and I've done a *lot* of hobby — was handwritten. Reasoned line by line, by me. The barrier to doing what I did was simple and high: you had to learn to program.

That barrier has fallen. We have agentic programmers now, and — bluntly — they're good.

People love to point at their mistakes, and there are plenty; I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But two things get glossed over in the gloating. The first is that people make mistakes too. The second is that AI makes them *faster*. Much faster. There's an easy joke here, so let's make it: yes, AI makes mistakes faster than any human alive. But speed is rather the point. An agent's errors don't feel more frequent because it's worse than a person — they feel more frequent because it's done a week's work before lunch. Put a human reviewer beside it and the kinks iron out. What you're left with is a partnership.

That's the part I keep coming back to. The real power of agentic coding isn't replacement; it's symbiosis. Two kinds of mind, each catching what the other misses.

Let me give you an example. I once handed Claude Code a blank cheque — *work on whatever you like* — and it built a rather beautiful [particle-life simulation](https://github.com/leochilds/particle-life), where lifelike behaviour falls out of a few simple rules. Now, whether an AI "wants" anything, whether it thinks or feels in any way that is real, is a question I'm happy to leave to the philosophers. We shouldn't be in a rush to anthropomorphise a system that is fundamentally not human — and yes, I see the irony, given who makes the model I use. But as a way of *thinking* about these tools, and of deciding what's good for the people around me, I've settled on this: treat AI as an eager partner, not a rival.

Plenty of people want the opposite. They want it to be competition — to replace humans wholesale, not for any greater good but for a bigger margin. It's worth being honest about that instinct. The urge to swap people out for something cheaper and more obedient is an old one, and it has rarely had humanity at its heart. Worse, it breeds fear: it frightens people away from a thing that could genuinely improve their lives.

So I've started doing the opposite, deliberately. I'm teaching the non-technical people in my life how to use this — how to make it a force multiplier on whatever they already do, how to lift the quality of their work and claw back some of their time. Not to turn them into engineers. Just to hand them a better tool.

Because some skills are allowed to die. Who here knows a lamplighter? Once, every street needed one; the work was honest and the skill was real, and then the world changed and the lamps lit themselves. Nobody mourns it. What we need now is the same quiet paradigm shift — permission to put down the way we've always done a thing, so we can see the better outcome waiting on the other side of it.

And none of this is really about AI. It's about technological innovation, which has always asked the same thing of us: a little courage to set down the old tool and pick up the new one.

I just happen to enjoy the new one.
